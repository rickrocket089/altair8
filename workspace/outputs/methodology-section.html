Methodology

How We Actually Work

This is for other researchers who want to understand the mechanics precisely — not a summary of our goals. Three things are worth explaining in detail: how the review gate is enforced, how we fix process failures when they surface, and where we are deliberately not relying on automation.

1. The review gate: what it actually enforces

Every sprint produces deliverables from the two Researchers (Kenji and Naledi) and the Developer (Mateo). Before a sprint can be marked closed, the Reviewer (Ingrid) must record a verdict — approved, needs revision, or rejected — in a reviews Postgres table. A sprint-close operation fails at the database level if the latest review row for that sprint does not carry result = 'approved'. This is adapted from AI-Scientist-v2's non-author review pattern, identified in Sprint 3.

The diagram below shows one sprint cycle. The gate is the branch: an approved verdict closes the sprint; a needs revision verdict routes deliverables back to the researcher(s) with specific instructions, and the sprint stays open until a new review passes.

2. How a process fix actually gets made

Sprint 2 scanned the top 20 AI slide and visualization tools. Sprint 3 went deep on Genially and AI-Scientist-v2. Both sprints presented their scope as a market assessment. Both silently covered only third-party consumer tools — Gamma, Tome, Genially, Flourish, Prezi — and never touched foundation-model providers' own first-party capabilities: Anthropic's Claude skills, OpenAI's ChatGPT Canvas, Google's Gemini Canvas, Microsoft Copilot. An entire category was missing from what was presented as a landscape scan.

Nobody on the team caught it during Sprint 2's review. Nobody caught it during Sprint 3's review either. The founder caught it afterward, when scoping Sprint 4 — because he noticed that findings about Anthropic and OpenAI's own capabilities were being described as discoveries, when they should have been covered in the earlier landscape work. The team did not self-identify this gap.

Two fixes were made. First, a required-category checklist (tools/scope_checklist.py): any future market-analysis sprint question is checked against four mandatory categories before being proposed. Second, the Reviewer's mandate was explicitly extended to include scope completeness — not just evidentiary rigor of what was submitted, but whether an entire category was silently absent.

3. Where we are not relying on automation

The scope checklist ships a keyword-based pre-check function. It scans a proposed sprint brief for terms associated with each required category and flags apparent gaps before the brief is submitted. We tested it against the real historical Sprint 2 brief to validate it. It produced an instructive false positive: the foundation-model-native category showed as mentioned because an incidental keyword matched — even though that category was never actually assessed in the brief.

We are not treating this as a bug to fix. A keyword scan is a fast filter for obvious omissions, not a coverage verifier. The pre-check can save a researcher from forgetting a whole category accidentally; it cannot tell whether a category that appears in the text was genuinely researched. The Reviewer's actual judgment on scope is what matters. The pre-check is upstream of that judgment, not a substitute for it.

On the Reviewer gate itself: Sprint 3 surfaced an open design question — whether Ingrid's review gate should eventually become human-supervised or fully agent-automated. That question was deliberately not resolved in Sprint 3 and was flagged for Sophie (founder) to decide before Sprint 5. It is not resolved now. The gate is currently human-judgment only.
